Assemble an evaluation candidate

Bring normalized records together without turning uncertainty into a silent guess.

The idea

Identity matching tells us which records refer to the same player. Boundary normalization gives those records stable meaning. Candidate assembly uses both results to decide whether the system can create one quarterback candidate for a slate.

Normalized salary, stats, and matchup records
↓
Candidate assembler
↓
Candidate, review item, or explicit rejection
Boundary rule:
Assembly decides whether the records can form a candidate. Evaluation decides what a candidate is worth.

Three outcomes

  • Pass: create the candidate and send it to the evaluation policy. Unknown values remain unknown when the policy owns that decision.
  • Hold: preserve an unresolved identity for review. Never make a quiet cross-source guess.
  • Drop: reject inputs that cannot form a valid candidate, such as an invalid required salary.

Practice the decision

Choose the next action for each case. Start guided. When you can get five correct, switch to blind mode. The labels are intentionally short: you must retrieve the reason, not recognize it from the button text.

Transfer check

Suppose a quarterback has matching salary and matchup records, but no passing attempts. The normalized value remains unknown. Which component decides whether that makes the candidate unevaluable, and why is that decision not the assembler's?

Primary source

Read the Data Contract Specification, especially its treatment of data quality and contract expectations. Read it alongside Martin Fowler's Anti-Corruption Layer: external variation belongs at the boundary, while application rules stay owned by the application.

Done when

You can look at normalized source records and say whether to pass, hold, or drop them, then name the component that owns the next decision.

Ask follow-up questions whenever a case feels ambiguous. Explain which case made you hesitate and why.
